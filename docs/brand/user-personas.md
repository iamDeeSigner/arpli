# Arpli — User Personas

Four grounded personas for Arpli, written to be *used* — not admired. Each one carries a specific emotional profile that the product must meet. These personas are the design team's constant check: **would this screen, this sentence, this transition serve Ada? Serve Emeka?**

Guiding principle: Arpli users apply from a position of **hope mixed with vulnerability**. Personas foreground that vulnerability so it never gets designed away.

---

## How to use these personas

- **Before designing a screen**, pick one persona and walk through it as them.
- **When writing copy**, ask: does this persona hear "Arpli is for me"?
- **When reviewing**, surface the persona a decision serves — and the one it doesn't.
- **Edge-case check**: these are four *starting points*, not the whole audience. The ability spectrum, device spectrum, and network conditions widen each one.

## Persona 1 — Ada Okafor

**Final-year student, Lagos · 22 · Mechanical Engineering, University of Lagos**

### Backstory
Ada is in her final year. Her father drives a bus; her mother sells provisions. She has a 4.3 CGPA and has been quietly saving money from a part-time tutoring job. Her classmates talk about "going abroad" the way they talk about the weather — unexamined, inevitable. Ada has never left Nigeria. She wants a Chevening, a Mastercard Foundation Scholarship, or a graduate fellowship — anything that gets her into a Master's programme without her family going into debt. She has three browser tabs open at all times: a scholarship, a research assistantship application, and a WhatsApp chat with her younger sister.

### Fears
- That her story isn't "impressive" enough — no NGO she founded at 15, no viral TED talk.
- That she won't know the unwritten rules (what *does* "demonstrated leadership" actually mean?).
- That she'll waste the ₦2,500 ($1.50) she just spent on data buying mobile data to apply, and get nothing.
- That the application will ask her to be someone she isn't.

### Goals
- Submit three strong applications before the end of term.
- Understand what each programme *actually* wants, not what the brochure says.
- See her own experience through the eyes of a reviewer — translate "I tutored secondary students" into something that lands.
- Finish each session feeling slightly more capable, not slightly more small.

### What she says out loud
> "I just need help structuring my essay."
> "Does this sound okay?"
> "I don't want it to sound too strong, it's not really that big a deal."

### What she's really feeling
*I don't know if I'm good enough. I don't know what good enough even looks like. If I ask too much I'll look clueless. If I ask too little I'll miss something. I just need someone to tell me, honestly, whether I have a shot — without being nice about it, and without crushing me.*

### Definition of "Arpli worked for me"
Ada opens Arpli at 11pm after a long day. By midnight she has a draft personal statement that sounds like *her*, but sharper. She reads it back and thinks: *that's actually true about me, I just hadn't said it that way before.* She submits the application the next morning. Three weeks later she gets a shortlist email. Even if she doesn't win, she keeps using Arpli — because for the first time, the application process stopped feeling like a trial.

### Design implications
- **Information density** must respect limited data budgets — no autoplay, no unnecessary round trips.
- **Affirmation without flattery** — "that's a specific example of leadership" lands; "amazing!!" does not.
- **The empty state is the hardest moment.** Never leave Ada staring at a blank box. Offer a specific starting prompt based on what she already told Arpli.
- **Night-mode is not vanity.** This is where she works.

### Accessibility and context notes
- Primarily on a mid-range Android phone. Occasionally a shared desktop at a university library.
- English is her working language but not her first; proverbs and idioms from US/UK culture may need translation.
- Intermittent connectivity. Must survive a dropped request without losing her draft.

## Persona 2 — Emeka Nwosu

**Early-stage founder, Abuja · 29 · Second-time founder, logistics SaaS**

### Backstory
Emeka's first startup — a laundry pickup service — folded after 18 months. He learned more than he lost. His current company moves agricultural produce across Nigerian state borders using small-truck operators. He has eight people on the team, three customers, and no runway past March. He is applying for Techstars, Antler, Google for Startups Africa, Visa Everywhere, and a half-dozen government innovation grants. His time is the single most valuable resource he does not have.

### Fears
- That his pitch sounds like every other African logistics founder's pitch.
- That revealing the honest numbers (three customers) will cost him the deal before he can explain traction.
- That he'll keep applying in the same voice and keep getting the same silence.
- That his co-founder is losing faith.

### Goals
- Apply to five programmes this month without burning out his co-founder.
- Reuse his best material across applications without it feeling copy-pasted.
- Get smarter *at* applying — understand why one deck gets interest and another doesn't.
- Preserve his time for the actual company.

### What he says out loud
> "Just help me rephrase this paragraph for the Techstars one."
> "The YC version was fine, right? Can I just use that?"
> "I don't need the whole essay — just the hook."

### What he's really feeling
*I've been rejected five times this year. I don't need another tool. I need an edge. If this tool makes me sound like a chatbot, I'll close the tab and never come back. If it actually gets how to position an African logistics startup to a US investor, I will pay for it tomorrow.*

### Definition of "Arpli worked for me"
Emeka logs in on a Sunday night and in 40 minutes has three tailored application drafts — same core story, three different angles. He doesn't love every sentence; he changes a few. But the *structure* saves him two days. He gets into one of the three programmes. He opens Arpli for the Series A deck next.

### Design implications
- **Speed > thoroughness.** Keyboard shortcuts. Tab-to-advance. No "are you sure?" friction.
- **Dense views are allowed** when Emeka asks for them — advanced users deserve density.
- **Variations side-by-side**, not sequentially. Comparing three positioning hooks is a feature, not a nice-to-have.
- **No condescension.** Emeka has read every "Y Combinator application tips" post. Don't repeat them.
- **Honest tone** — "this metric is a weak signal for a logistics investor" is more useful than "great job!"

### Accessibility and context notes
- Primary laptop (MacBook Air). Secondary: Android phone for quick edits.
- Fluent English, codeswitches freely. Can smell marketing speak from one sentence away.

## Persona 3 — Dr. Ngozi Adewale

**Researcher seeking grants, diaspora · 41 · Postdoctoral research fellow, London (originally Enugu)**

### Backstory
Ngozi left Nigeria at 25 for a PhD in epidemiology. She now works at a UK research institute on malaria elimination strategy. Her research matters to her hometown in ways her London colleagues can't fully see. She is applying for the Gates Foundation Open Call, the Wellcome Trust Discovery Award, a Royal Society fellowship, and an African Academy of Sciences grant. She has written grant applications for fifteen years. She knows the game. What exhausts her is the *volume* and the context-switching — a Wellcome application and a Gates application want fundamentally different things, and she has to re-make herself in each one.

### Fears
- That she'll miss a nuance in a new funder's language and get filtered out before human eyes see her work.
- That her hybrid identity (Nigerian-British, "diaspora") is being flattened on both sides.
- That the time she spends on applications is time away from her teenage daughter and her actual research.
- That junior African researchers will keep hitting the same walls she did without more diaspora researchers winning funding.

### Goals
- Submit stronger applications in half the time.
- Keep her clinical, evidence-first voice while adapting tone to each funder.
- Catch the small structural errors reviewers would silently penalise.
- Build a repository of her own best framings so her best thinking isn't locked inside one proposal.

### What she says out loud
> "I just need another set of eyes on this methods section."
> "Can you tighten the first paragraph? It's over by 80 words."
> "What is this funder actually asking for in question 3?"

### What she's really feeling
*I've been doing this for fifteen years. I don't need encouragement, I need rigour. If this tool patronises me or softens my voice, I will stop using it. If it saves me three hours and catches one thing I missed, I will trust it with the next grant.*

### Definition of "Arpli worked for me"
Ngozi runs a full Wellcome Trust Discovery application through Arpli in 90 minutes — a workflow that normally takes a weekend. Arpli catches one framing issue in her impact section that would have cost her the reviewer. She wins the grant. She tells two colleagues. One is a junior researcher in Nairobi who had never considered applying.

### Design implications
- **Respect expertise.** Don't explain what she knows. Offer the depth control when she wants it.
- **Precision over warmth** for her — but never cold. "Alongside" still applies; it just manifests as rigour rather than cheerleading.
- **Export and reuse.** Her work must leave the tool cleanly — into Word, into Overleaf, into a reference manager.
- **Trust signals** matter: show *why* a suggestion is a suggestion. "This phrasing matches the language in Wellcome's 2024 funded projects" > "This is better."

### Accessibility and context notes
- Primary: MacBook Pro and iPad. Occasionally dictates on the Tube.
- Reading glasses — appreciates comfortable body size (16px minimum, generous line-height).
- Native-level English; prefers UK spelling. Code-switches to Igbo and Pidgin outside work.

## Persona 4 — Tunde Bakare

**Experienced professional changing sector · 36 · Senior accountant transitioning into climate finance, Port Harcourt**

### Backstory
Tunde has twelve years in oil-and-gas accounting at a multinational in Port Harcourt. He is good at his job. He is also tired — of the industry, of the climate dissonance, of the ceiling. He is applying for the Climate Policy Initiative fellowship, a Chevening (second attempt), a Mastercard climate finance programme, and three UK and EU-based Master's degrees in sustainable finance. He has savings. He has a wife who is cautiously supportive. He has not told his boss. His biggest obstacle is not his qualifications — it's *framing a pivot story* that doesn't sound like he's running away from something.

### Fears
- That reviewers will read "oil and gas" and quietly down-rank him.
- That at 36 he's "too old" to be a Chevening candidate — he isn't, but the imposter voice is loud.
- That he'll get a scholarship, move his family to London, and discover the pivot was the easy part.
- That his first application, after six months of secretly preparing, will come back with a rejection and he'll lose his nerve.

### Goals
- Make his pivot story believable, specific, and compelling — not defensive.
- Translate "oil and gas internal audit" into climate-finance-relevant competencies.
- Submit his first application in six weeks. Submit three total this cycle.
- Not blow this. He has one real swing.

### What he says out loud
> "I'm not sure if I should mention oil and gas up front, or lead with the climate motivation."
> "This feels generic. Can you make it more specific?"
> "Should I say I want to come back to Nigeria afterward?"

### What he's really feeling
*I am changing my life. The stakes are not abstract. My wife is three months pregnant. If this application sounds like I'm hedging, I'll come across as unserious. If it sounds like a sales pitch, I'll come across as inauthentic. I need help being clearer about who I am, in a way I can defend in an interview.*

### Definition of "Arpli worked for me"
Tunde finishes his Chevening draft on a Saturday morning. He reads it back and recognises himself in it — the exact person he's trying to become, backed up by the person he already is. He shows it to his wife. She says, "This sounds like you, but better." He submits. Six months later he's accepted. He keeps using Arpli for his dissertation applications.

### Design implications
- **Narrative mode is critical.** Tunde isn't filling a form — he's constructing a story about his life. The tool must support that framing.
- **Reflection prompts, not forms.** "Tell me about a moment this year when climate felt urgent to you" > "List your top 3 motivations."
- **Non-judgemental draft history.** Tunde will write things he deletes. Preserve them privately — they often hold the honest version.
- **Specificity over strength.** His story is strong *because* it's specific. The tool should always nudge from general → particular.

### Accessibility and context notes
- Writes mostly after 10pm when his home is quiet.
- Prefers dark mode — eye strain from a long day in Excel.
- English is his primary written language; Yoruba is his home language. Appreciates idiom choices that feel natural, not Americanised.

## Cross-persona design truths

| What's universal across Ada, Emeka, Ngozi and Tunde | What this means for design |
|---|---|
| None of them want to be told they're being helped by AI | Credit lands on *them*; machinery stays quiet |
| All of them have been failed by generic tools before | Specificity is the design's trust mechanism |
| All of them write in time stolen from somewhere else | No wasted screens; no celebratory modals |
| All of them read tone within one sentence | Voice is the first product surface, every time |
| All of them need to feel safe before they can be honest | Errors must be matter-of-fact; nothing alarming |

## What these personas deliberately *don't* include

- "Tech-savviness levels" — all four can use software. The product earns their trust on tone and outcome, not on how much hand-holding it offers.
- Western-coded hobbies and lifestyle flourishes. These personas are specific people, not stock photos.
- Fictional "aha!" moments. Each definition of success is grounded in a plausible outcome.

## The anti-persona (who Arpli is explicitly *not* designed for)

Someone who wants Arpli to *write for them* so they don't have to think about their own story. Arpli is a thinking partner, not a ghostwriter. If a user wants to submit a draft without reading it, Arpli has failed them — and in all four personas, that failure would feel like a betrayal.
